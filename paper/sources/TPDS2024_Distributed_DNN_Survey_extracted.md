# A Survey on Performance Modeling and Prediction for Distributed DNN Training Zhenhua Guo, Yinan Tang  , Jidong Zhai  , Senior Member, IEEE , Tongtong Yuan  , Jian Jin  , Li Wang, Yaqian Zhao, and Rengang Li 

Abstract —The recent breakthroughs in large-scale DNN attract significant attention from both academia and industry toward distributed DNN training techniques. Due to the time-consuming and expensive execution process of large-scale distributed DNN training, it is crucial to model and predict the performance of distributed DNN training before its actual deployment, in order to optimize the design of distributed DNN training at low cost. This paper analyzes and emphasizes the importance of modeling and predicting the performance of distributed DNN training, catego-rizes and analyses the related state-of-the-art works, and discusses future challenges and opportunities for this research field. The objectives of this paper are twofold: first, to assist researchers in understanding and choosing suitable modeling and prediction tools for large-scale distributed DNN training, and second, to encourage researchers to propose more valuable research about performance modeling and prediction for distributed DNN training in the future. 

Index Terms —Distributed DNN training, distributed computing systems, large-scale machine learning, performance modeling.

II. B ACKGROUND AND MOTIVATIONS 

A. Why Modeling and Predicting the Performance for Distributed DNN Training 

Although distributed training can be automatically realized based on existing deep learning frameworks (e.g. Tensor-flow and Pytorch), it remains challenging to ensure optimal execution [15]. Suppose that researchers want to optimize dis-tributed training in a practical deployment system, it is necessary to make modifications to both the computing cluster and deep learning framework software, meanwhile developing a monitor. Furthermore, during the optimizing process, multiple iterations of distributed training are required to be executed on a large-scale cluster, which will inevitably incur significant computational resource usage, energy consumption, and expensive labor costs. By contrast, as depicted in Fig. 1, performance modeling and prediction provide an alternative approach where distributed training is virtually modeled instead of running on a real com-puting cluster, aiming to predict the time cost or the running trace of distributed training. By decoupling the optimization process from the underlying runtime platform through modeling and prediction, researchers can explore parallel strategies, comput-ing cluster configurations, and even more design spaces of dis-tributed training at low cost, while avoiding making complex and time-consuming modifications to the hardware and software. Although there have been some survey articles about dis-tributed training [16], [17], [18], they mainly focus on the de-ployment methods or training strategies, with limited coverage on the performance modeling and prediction studies. In order to fill this gap, this article provides a comprehensive survey on performance modeling and prediction for distributed DNN training. 

B. Main Design Spaces Should Be Modeled 

In this section, we briefly introduce the main design spaces of distributed training as exhibited in Fig. 1, which have a great impact on distributed DNN training. 

1) Parallel Strategies: Parallel strategies are used to allocate computing tasks to GPUs (or computing nodes) in a computing cluster, aiming to enable multiple GPUs to perform efficient DNN training collaboratively. Common parallel strategies can be categorized into Data Parallelism (DP), Model Parallelism (MP), and Hybrid Parallelism (HP). DP is primarily designed for distributed training scenarios with large training datasets. In DP, each computing node within a computing cluster maintains a replica of DNN and its own subset of training data. Each computing node independently performs the forward and backward propagation, and the gradients are then accumulated for model parameter synchronization. There are two common parallel modes for DP, including centralized mode and decentralized mode. In the centralized mode, the workers transmit their computed gradients to the Parameter Servers (PS) [19] and receive the most recent synchronized model state in return. In the decentralized mode, the parameter servers are removed, and weight gradients are exchanged among all nodes through all-reduce collective communications [20], [21]. The optional synchronization strategy of model param-eters mainly includes Bulk Synchronous Parallel (BSP) [22], Staleness Synchronous Parallel (SSP) [23], and Asynchronous Parallel (ASP) [24]. Although SSP and ASP can accelerate distributed training to some extent, they would inevitably cause a decrease in the accuracy of DNN compared with BSP [25]. Therefore, BSP is currently the most widely accepted and used synchronization strategy in the industry. In terms of MP, the DNN model is partitioned, aiming to address the issue that the DNN model is too large to be trained on a single computing node. This can be achieved by either partitioning the tensors (also called tensor MP) or performing multi-stage partitioning along the depth of DNN (also called pipeline MP). For tensor MP, the weight matrixes are divided and allocated to multiple computing nodes. Each computing node performs computations using its allocated part of the weight matrix with the collected input, and subsequently broadcasts its partial outputs to other computing nodes. For pipeline MP, the input data is further divided into micro-batches [26], and then sequentially inputted into a computing cluster with a pipeline fashion. Research such as Dapple [27] and Chimera [28] have further proposed various effective scheduling algorithms for pipeline MP. In addition, Expert Parallelism (EP) [29] designed for Mixture-of-Expert (MoE) models [30] can be regarded as a variant of MP. By leveraging parallel strategies of DP and MP with dif-ferent dimensions, HP is able to further optimize distributed training [31], [32]. Taking GPT-3 [6] as an example, its HP-based training strategy is shown in Fig. 2. To achieve efficient HP and reduce the difficulty for researchers in designing HP strategies, some approaches propose automated parallelism [33] to search for the optimal HP strategies [34], [35], [36], [37]. These methods typically rely on inserted modeling or prediction technology for distributed training, aiming to guide the opti-mization objectives of the HP strategy searching algorithm. For instance, Alpa [35] models the intra-operator execution cost and the inter-operator parallelization latency, then searches for the optimal HP strategy by minimizing both of them. Different parallel strategies can be applied to adapt different training scenarios, yielding varying training accelera-tion effects. Therefore, designing an optimal distributed training parallel strategy has emerged as a central concern in the field of distributed training. 2) Computation Optimization: Computation optimization refers to optimizing the computation processes, which offers extensive opportunities for exploration. On one hand, it is pos-sible to optimize the allocation for the sub-computation tasks among the inter computing nodes [38]. On the other hand, for the computation process inside one computing node, several compu-tation factors such as the training-precision settings during train-ing [39] and the batch size used [40] can all impact the training performance. Moreover, the works for optimizing computing operators [41] and AI compilers [42] can also be seen as the computation optimizations at the software level. With the help of performance modeling and prediction for distributed training, design spaces of the computation optimization can be explored at a low cost, avoiding the time-consuming development and actual execution. 

3) Communication Optimization: Numerous research works have demonstrated that communication has become a major performance bottleneck for distributed training [17]. To optimize the collective communication operators during distributed train-ing such as all-reduce, reduce-scatter, and all-gather, several re-cent studies proposed to accelerate the operators themselves. For example, [43] proposed hierarchical all-reduce communication and tree-structured all-reduce communication, [44] presented topology-aware collective communication, [45] suggested com-pression techniques for reducing the communication volume of collective communication, and so on. Besides, optimizing the splitting and fusion strategies of collective communication operators (e.g. the tensor fusion in Horovod [20]) is also a promising approach [46]. As a result, communication optimiza-tion is an extensively studied design space, and it is also a focus of many performance modeling and prediction works for distributed DNN training. 

4) Memory and Data Loading: On one hand, memory op-timization enables GPU to train larger models with limited memory, which can save a lot of costs and has become a key research focus in recent years. For example, ZeRO [47] proposes a data DP-based training approach that splits model parameters, gradients, and optimizer states among multiple GPUs, achieving significant memory savings with only 1.5 times additional com-munication overhead, and its subsequent work [48], [49] goes further in tapping into CPU memory and NVMe storage. Check-mate [50] employs recomputation techniques [51], [52] to trade off computation for additional memory space. Data encoding and compression are used in the work [53] to reduce memory consumption during training. In recent years, new protocols such as NVLink-C2C [54] and CXL [55] also fall under mem-ory optimization by designing consistent multi-device memory interconnect protocols, which can help realize collaborative caching [56] to enhance the performance of distributed training, especially for DP that requires a large amount of memory. On the other hand, the process of loading training data is also a key design space [57]. Designers need to consider how to effectively read training data from the storage (e.g., Hadoop Distribute File System [58]) to ensure that I/O does not limit the training speed. Besides, the training performance is usually affected by the data stall phenomenon in the data pipeline (in-cluding reading, preprocessing, shuffling training samples, and assembling mini-batches) [59], which involves the complicated design and optimization of both software and hardware [60]. In short, both memory optimization and data loading accel-eration are challenging for actual deployment, so it is better to predict and quantify their effects using modeling and prediction technologies. 

5) Cluster Design: As the underlying infrastructure for dis-tributed training, the cluster design is one of the most critical design spaces that affects the training performance. However, designing and optimizing cluster design in real-world systems often involves time-consuming modifications and restructuring of cluster architecture, as well as hardware migrations and changes. In contrast, using performance modeling and prediction for distributed training allows researchers to explore the cluster design in a sandbox environment, aiding in exploring cluster design much more easily. For the technology of cluster design, regarding the com-puting nodes, works such as DGX [61] have demonstrated the importance of designing specialized servers for distributed training. Besides, several studies have introduced new hardware architectures such as Field Programmable Gate Arrays (FPGA) to further optimize distributed training. For the design of in-terconnection between cluster nodes, numerous works focus on designing new cluster topologies. For example, industry clusters like Facebook’s Zion platforms [62] and Google’s TPU platforms [63], as well as academic studies such as [64], all try to optimize the collaborative efficiency between computing nodes at the topology level. Unlink traditional topologies such as tree-based data center architectures and 3D-torus architecture, cluster architectures are also promising to be extended to higher-dimensional (e.g. 4D/5D) interconnects to enhance the network capacity between computing nodes [65]. Moreover, the choice of network transmission protocols (such as RoCE, Infiniband, TCP/IP) also impacts the performance of distributed training. 2466 IEEE TRANSACTIONS ON PARALLEL AND DISTRIBUTED SYSTEMS, VOL. 35, NO. 12, DECEMBER 2024

6) Collaborative Design: Different design spaces may also have mutual influences, which presents further challenges. For example, in some multidimensional cluster topologies such as 3D-torus, a collective communication operator can be further optimized by dividing it into multiple stages, aiming to effi-ciently utilize the multidimensional network resources [66]. As the work [15] illustrated in Fig. 3, communication and com-putation can also be co-optimized to realize overlap [67], [68], while more similar co-optimized can be achieved by taking into account the barriers and degree of inter-node and intra-node parallelisms, model sharding, and pipelining designs, etc. In addition, because distributed training is sensitive to communica-tion, processes such as I/O, P2P, and collective communication can be jointly designed with parallel strategies [18], aiming to avoid mutual interference. Such collaborative designs are effective but hard to design and realize because the designer needs to solve complex trade-offs among different parallelism, communication, and computation strategies, etc., under a given training configuration. In terms of the above design spaces, as well as other design spaces not mentioned in this section, the cost and difficulty of ex-ploring them in practical distributed training are significant. The studies of performance modeling and prediction for distributed training were proposed with the explicit purpose of addressing this challenge, thereby helping researchers in the exploration of intricate design spaces for distributed training. III. P ERFORMANCE MODELING AND PREDICTION FOR DISTRIBUTED DNN T RAINING According to the methodology of modeling distributed train-ing, the performance modeling and prediction works can be classified into the following three categories: 

##  Analytical models: utilizing analytical models or formulas to model the execution of distributed training, then predict its performance. 

##  Graph-based models: using graph-based models to de-scribe the execution process of distributed training. 

##  Execution-driven models: combining simulation tools or real computing platforms to emulate the execution of dis-tributed training. This section surveys and introduces the state-of-the-art works, according to the categories above.

2) Summaries and Insights: In summary, it can be concluded that most analytical models are targeted towards DP, with only a few analytical models being applied to Tensor MP and Pipeline MP. The main advantages of analytical models lie in their ability to quickly and efficiently predict the computational time or throughput of distributed training . However, analytical models have the following shortcomings: 

##  Most analytical models have to introduce or profile a large number of difficult-to-obtain variables , ultimately leading to great difficulty in deployment when the execution of distributed training is complex (e.g., the studies designed for modeling ASP [88], [89]). 

##  The generality and expansibility of analytical models are not strong . When the explored modification of design space (especially parallel strategies) affects the distributed train-ing workflow, the analytical models must be redesigned to adapt to the newly proposed distributed training methods.

1) State-of-the-Arts: Earlier, Paleo [90] proposed by Qi et al. uses a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) to model distributed training. Each vertex in DAG represents a computing operator executed at the allocated computing node, including computa-tion and I/O read/write, whose execution times are respectively estimated using the operator’s computational complexity (i.e. Floating Point Operations, FLOPs) divided by the computa-tion performance (i.e. Floating Point Operations Per Second, FLOPS) and operator memory consumption divided by the memory bandwidth of the computing node. The communication processes in distributed training are not explicitly depicted in DAG, so Paleo directly models the communication process using data amount divided by bandwidth once communication occurs between computing nodes. The edges of the DAG represent the dependency relationships between the operators, which means the directed vertex will not be executed until the previous vertex is completed. In this way, when parallelism appears, Paleo inserts synchronous boundaries into the DAG, and the time consumption of the parallel processes can be obtained by using the maximum time consumption of all DAG branches before the boundaries. Paleo finally achieves good prediction accuracy as shown in Table II, and its graph-based modeling approach has become the baseline for many subsequent studies. Jia et al. proposed FlexFlow [93], which relies on a built-in graph-based model to automatically search for the optimal par-allel strategies. Based on inputs of computing graphs, cluster topologies, and founded parallel strategies, FlexFlow models the entire distributed training by constructing a task graph, where its vertexes stand for both computational and commu-nication processes, and its edges represent their dependency. FlexFlow uses the same data-divide-by-bandwidth model in Paleo [90] to calculate the time consumption of communication, but the computational time is obtained by actually executing and profiling the operators. In this way, the running time of all vertexes in the task graph can be obtained, so the training throughput of distributed training under a given parallel strat-egy can be predicted. Furthermore, on the basis of the above model, FlexFlow is enabled to use the Metropolis-Hastings algo-rithm [105] to search for parallel strategies and find the optimal one. Zhu et al. proposed DayDream [95], which uses a fine-grained graph-based model to describe distributed training, mainly targeting all-reduce based DP. DayDream uses CU-PIT [106] to profile the GPU tasks, CPU tasks, data read-ing tasks, and communication tasks during the execution of distributed training. Then, DayDream uses the dependencies obtained by CUPIT to construct a dependency graph at the kernel level. After building such a dependency graph, DayDream is enabled to predict the execution time of distributed training. Furthermore, suppose some methods are trying to optimize the distributed training in lower levels (using methods such as Priority-Based Parameter Propagation [107], FusedAdam Opti-mizer [108], etc.), the optimization effect can be predicted and verified without actual deployment using DayDream, by insert-ing or deleting the vertexes, and modifying the edges between vertexes. 2470 IEEE TRANSACTIONS ON PARALLEL AND DISTRIBUTED SYSTEMS, VOL. 35, NO. 12, DECEMBER 2024 Similarly, Hu et al. proposed dPRO [98], which relies on a graph-based model to predict and optimize the performance of DP-based distributed training. DPRO consists of three modules, including a profiler, a replayer, and an optimizer. The profiler is used to collect operator-level traces on large-scale clusters, including the operators of computation and communication, and then establishes a Data Flow Graph (DFG) to describe distributed training. More specifically, the DFG consists of a local sub-DFG built by the profiled computational operators and a global sub-DFG built according to the profiled communication operators, both of which aim to draw the dependencies between the operators. Then, in its replayer, based on the generated DFG, dPRO uses the Kahn algorithm [109] to replay the trace of distributed training and predict the execution time cost. Finally, in the optimizer, dPRO identifies the bottlenecks in the DFG, and tries to relieve the bottlenecks by fusing or splitting the operators and tensors in the DFG. The optimized DFG is iter-atively replayed in the replayer, aiming to search for a better DFG. According to the experiment results, dPRO can provide a maximum acceleration of 3.48 times based on the optimizer. Another graph-based work is DistSim [99] proposed by Lu et al. which can be used to predict the detailed timeline of distributed training in each participating computing node. Based on the input DNN model and parallel strategy of HP-based distributed training, DistSim can generate a sub-computation graph for each involved computing node. Then, based on these sub-computation graphs, DistSim converts the graphs into mul-tiple computational and communication events, and constructs the timeline of distributed training by profiled data. DistSim emphasizes that profiling is not required for all the events (or operators), due to the large number of repetitions in the computation and communication process. Therefore, DistSim deduplicates identical events to obtain a set of events that require profiling, and only profiles the events within the set, leading to a lower cost for predicting the entire timeline of distributed training. To better model the runtime characteristics, Duan et al. pro-posed Proteus [102], which uses a policy tree to describe the parallel strategy and relies on an execution graph to model the distributed training. According to the user-provided policy tree which defined the fine-grained parallel policy for the tensors and operators, Proteus uses a compiler to generate the execution graph, by first generating tensors and computation operators, and then inserting communication operations, data dependency, and control dependency. Proteus is capable of taking into account bandwidth conflicts and computation-communication overlaps, which are two common runtime effects of distributed training. model [110]. Finally, Proteus can output predicted throughput and memory consumption of the distributed training, achieving approximately 3% of predicting error. TAG [104] proposed by Zhang et al. is introduced to search for the optimal partial data parallelism [93]. Since this work proposed a strategy searching method based on the combination of Graph Neural Network (GNN) [111] and MCTS [112], TAG also relies on a graph-based model to describe the distributed training process. More specifically, for the aim of reducing com-munication, TAG design a GNN-based reinforcement learning approach to guide MCTS, finding the possible cut in the com-putation graph then replacing the original all-reduce or PS com-munication as sufficient factor broadcasting [113]. After such an optimization, TAG transforms the optimized computation graph into a distributed training graph, and inserts necessary auxiliary operators to ensure the authenticity of the graph-based model. Then, according to the dependency described by the distributed training graph, TAG sets up a FIFO-queues base virtual runtime to evaluate the optimized computation graph using the profiled data. In this way, TAG can optimize the communication overhead and speed up the distributed training, both for unseen cluster topology and DNN models without retraining. 

2) Summaries and Insights: Compared with the analytical models, graph-based models can model more details about the workflow of distributed training , where the reasons are as follows: 

##  Graphs offer the ability to model dependencies , so any modification on the graph-based model can be more cor-rectly mapped to the workflow changes of distributed train-ing, leading to better accuracy. 

##  On graph-based models, distributed training can be mod-eled and optimized through making graph operations. For instance, dPRO [98] uses vertex fusing and splitting to achieve training acceleration, while TAG [104] relies on graph cuts to find possible optimization. However, graph-based models also have their limitations: 

##  As shown in Table II, most works use profiled data for computation and communication modeling, which requires actual devices or even an expensive real distributed train-ing environment .

##  The size and complexity of the generated graph increase rapidly as the scale of the modeled computing cluster increases , posing significant challenges to predicting speed and memory consumption.

The greatest advantage of execution models lies in their ability to model fine-grained design spaces, and their high sensitivity to various design parameters or variations within these design spaces. By leveraging the characteristics of execution models, researchers are enabled to explore or optimize multiple design spaces such as cluster design, memory and data loading, computation, com-munication, and even their collaborative design. Several existing studies [132], [133] have successfully utilized execution models to optimize their system design for distributed training, which well validates the effectiveness of execution models.

C HALLENGES AND FUTURE OPPORTUNITIES Despite the numerous efforts dedicated to modeling and pre-dicting the performance of distributed DNN training, there are still several limitations in existing research, resulting in areas that can be further improved. This section aims to discuss the current challenges and future opportunities, taking into account the findings from the existing state-of-the-art works. 

A. Fine-Grained Modeling of Computing and Memory 1) Challenges: As research on distributed training using heterogeneous computing [134], [135] continues to increase and become commercially viable, there is an urgent need for fine-grained and high-precision modeling for computing nodes. Furthermore, innovations in memory system architecture, such as memory pooling [136] and memory disaggregation [55], also present new challenges for modeling the computing process. However, existing modeling or simulation methods that use the constant value of FLOPS without considering memory often suffer from deviations, while profiling-based methods inevitably require an actual hardware environment. As a result, the exist-ing methods can hardly achieve fine-grained modeling for the computing and memory of distributed training. 

2) Opportunities: One solution is to integrate the model of distributed training with existing GPU architecture simulators such as GPGPU-Sim [137] and Gem5-APU [138], or com-bine memory simulators such as DRAMsim3 [139], both of which help construct high-precision modeling of GPU comput-ing architecture. Astra-sim [66] provides a good example by combining Scale-sim [127] or execution traces [130] to model distributed training, allowing Astra-sim to work more similarly with the real hardware environment in terms of computing and memory. Nevertheless, it is important to note that those fine-grained simulators usually operate at the cycle level, so the simulation speed is also a key factor that needs to be further considered. 

B. Support for Complex Network Topologies 1) Challenges: The network topology between computing nodes has a significant impact on distributed training. However, existing studies have limited capabilities in modeling network topologies. Most existing works [90], [102] rely on simple analytical models (e.g., data volume/bandwidth, α + β models) or profiling data (e.g. Daydream [95] and dPRO [98]), which can hardly model the network behaviors such as congestion control. As a result, if researchers want to evaluate the impact of network topologies on distributed training and explore the optimal interconnection topology of computing nodes before deployment, existing works may not meet their requirements. 

2) Opportunities: Fortunately, many existing network sim-ulation tools already support customizing network topologies. For example, Simgrid [118] allows the description of custom topologies with bandwidth, delay, and routing information in XML files. Ns-3 [129] and OPNET [140] also allow fine-grained modifications of network topologies in simulations. Therefore, it is possible to integrate the network communication processes 2474 IEEE TRANSACTIONS ON PARALLEL AND DISTRIBUTED SYSTEMS, VOL. 35, NO. 12, DECEMBER 2024 of distributed training into mature network simulation tools, enhancing the modeling capabilities for network topologies. It is important to note that the choice of network simulator also determines the scalability of modeling distributed training. A coarser-grained network simulator allows for better scalability, while a fine-grained network simulator is more suitable for modeling small-scale distributed training. 

C. Unified Standard Benchmark for Performance Prediction 1) Challenges: Although existing works claimed their high accuracy and had reported their experimental verification, both the methods of evaluation and the DNNs used for comparison are different, making it difficult to make cross-comparisons for these works. In this case, there is an urgent need for a standardized and unified benchmark to evaluate the performance of different modeling and predicting approaches. 

2) Opportunities: For the establishment of such a bench-mark, reference can be taken from the benchmarking works in the field of AI, such as MLPerf [141], to create a uni-fied set of basic tests that include various distributed training configurations and training tasks with commonly used DNNs. Then, the constructed benchmark can be used to evaluate the accuracy and execution performance of different performance modeling and predicting works. Building a unified benchmark is helpful for the users to choose more accurate and efficient performance-predicting tools, and it can also assist researchers in continuously iterating and optimizing their works in this field. Chakra [130] provides a good method to generate execution traces of distributed training for benchmarking. 

D. Adaptability to More Design Spaces 1) Challenges: As AI technology continues to evolve, innovation and optimization for design spaces will keep emerg-ing. In this case, the performance modeling and prediction studies also need to keep up with the changes, by designing and realizing adaptability to new design spaces, which means they should support correctly working after opening, closing, or modifying a part of their design spaces in the model. 

2) Opportunities: In terms of adaptability to new tasks, graph-based and execution-driven models significantly show their advantage. For example, by changing the interconnections between nodes and edges in existing graph-based models (e.g. Paleo [90]), the design space of a new parallel strategy can be expressed, without making modifications to other design spaces such as computation and communication. Leveraging modular characteristics, the recent execution-driven models also support inserting, removing, or switching components of their models [66], [125]. In contrast, the analytical models can hardly adapt to new distributed training, due to their fixed perfor-mance modeling formula. To enhance the adaptability of existing works, the performance modeling and predicting works need to provide users with flexible and configurable interfaces to configure the design spaces, without causing modeling failure. It is also promising to combine well-verified analytical models with the other two, especially when modeling design spaces such as computation or communication.

E. AI-Enabled Modeling, Predicting, and Dynamic Optimizing 1) Challenges: Due to the vast design spaces and numerous factors of distributed training, taking into account all the details through fine-grained modeling would improve prediction accu-racy but severely degrade the prediction speed. In such cases, modeling large-scale distributed training would become notably slow, and optimizing distributed training during runtime is also impractical, both of which limit the application of the modeling and prediction studies. 

2) Opportunities: To address this problem, insights can be drawn from several simulation works that incorporated AI mod-els as the foundation for modeling or simulation [142], [143], [144], especially in the field of simulating large-scale computing or communication systems. Following this idea, as shown in Fig. 4, by training an AI model based on the collected datasets from a real-world system, a well-trained AI model can serve as a black-box model, replacing several compute-intensive modules to achieve efficient and accurate performance predicting. For example, in the work DNNemu [119], GBDT is used to pre-dict the computational process, which provides good insights for AI-based modeling. Researchers can choose to replace the intermediate modules they are less concerned about using AI models, while utilizing high-fidelity and interpretable models for the modules they are more interested in. Furthermore, AI is promising to achieve dynamic optimiza-tion for distributed training. Because distributed training pro-duces highly repetitive patterns at fine granularity, it becomes possible to predict its real-time performance by profiling a few training iterations. Then, complementary to prediction, AI-driven dynamic optimization approaches can be applied to boost the performance of distributed training. For instance, DL 2

[145] employs reinforcement learning to dynamically optimize the configuration of each training task, Spotlight [146] solves the device placement problem of distributed training by combining Markov decision process and reinforcement learning. Although these existing works can hardly optimize complex distributed training strategies such as HP, they have proven that AI-driven approaches are effective and fast enough to achieve real-time optimization for distributed training, rather than pre-computed before the actual execution. This paper first introduces the background and significance of performance modeling and prediction for distributed DNN training, and then discusses the main design spaces of distributed training. Subsequently, the cutting-edge related works are classified into three cate-gories, including analytical models, graph-based models, and execution-driven models, and then the key ideas, strengths, and weaknesses of the different categories are further introduced. Additionally, this article also aims to motivate researchers to propose more efficient and practical research outcomes for modeling and predicting the performance of distributed DNN training, thereby promoting the development and evolution of AI technology.
